\begin{abstract}
Robot self-modeling---the ability of a robot to autonomously learn an internal representation of its own morphology, kinematics, and dynamics from sensory data---is a foundational capability for resilient autonomous systems. A robot equipped with an accurate self-model can detect damage, plan collision-free motions, and adapt to morphological changes without human intervention. This review surveys the robot self-modeling literature, tracing its evolution from symbolic estimation-exploration approaches through behavioral adaptation strategies to modern visual self-models built on neural implicit representations and 3D Gaussian Splatting. We examine the neural rendering foundations that enable these methods, including Neural Radiance Fields and 3D Gaussian Splatting, and their emerging applications in robotic manipulation, digital twin construction, and sim-to-real transfer. We further analyze the critical challenges of continual learning and the stability-plasticity dilemma that arise when self-models must be updated post-damage, reviewing solutions from elastic weight consolidation to sparse evolutionary training. Finally, we identify key open challenges (real-time adaptation on edge hardware, scaling to high degrees of freedom, and bridging the simulation-to-reality gap) and outline promising future research directions for the field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
% ============================================================

Autonomous robots deployed in unstructured, remote, or hazardous environments---planetary rovers, disaster-response systems, deep-sea manipulators, and humanoid assistants---face a persistent challenge: they will sustain physical damage. When a manipulator's linkage bends, a legged robot loses a limb, or servo backlash accumulates beyond tolerance, the divergence between the robot's internal kinematic model and its physical reality leads to catastrophic control failure. Traditional robotic systems rely on predefined kinematic models, typically encoded as Unified Robot Description Format (URDF) files, which become invalid under such circumstances. The field of \textit{robot self-modeling} addresses this fundamental vulnerability by enabling robots to autonomously learn and maintain accurate internal representations of their own morphology from sensory data.

The concept of a machine that understands its own body has roots in both robotics and cognitive science. In biological systems, body schema---the brain's internal representation of the body's spatial configuration---is continuously updated through sensorimotor experience and enables rapid adaptation to injury \cite{bongard2006resilient}. Replicating this capability in artificial systems requires solving several intertwined challenges: choosing appropriate representational formats, collecting self-observation data efficiently, learning from limited samples, maintaining model fidelity as the body changes, and achieving computational speeds compatible with real-time control.

This review surveys the state of the art in robot self-modeling, with a particular focus on recent advances driven by neural rendering techniques, specifically Neural Radiance Fields (NeRFs) and 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), that have transformed the field's capabilities. We trace the evolution through three distinct eras: the \textit{symbolic era} of estimation-exploration (Section~\ref{sec:foundations}), the \textit{visual era} of neural implicit representations (Section~\ref{sec:visual_selfmodel}), and the emerging \textit{Gaussian splatting era} that promises real-time, high-fidelity self-models deployable on embedded hardware (Section~\ref{sec:3dgs_robotics}). We further examine the critical supporting topics of neural rendering (Section~\ref{sec:neural_rendering}), the sim-to-real gap (Section~\ref{sec:sim2real}), damage recovery (Section~\ref{sec:damage_recovery}), continual learning (Section~\ref{sec:continual_learning}), and enabling techniques (Section~\ref{sec:enabling}), before synthesizing open challenges and future directions (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Foundations of Robot Self-Modeling}
\label{sec:foundations}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Symbolic Self-Models and Active Exploration}

The foundational work in robot self-modeling is Bongard, Zykov, and Lipson's ``Resilient Machines Through Continuous Self-Modeling'' \cite{bongard2006resilient}, which demonstrated a physical four-legged robot (8 motorized joints, 8 joint angle sensors, 2 tilt sensors) that autonomously inferred its own morphology through self-directed exploration. The system executed three algorithmic components in a continuous loop: (1)~a \textit{modeling} phase that synthesized 15 candidate self-models using stochastic optimization over a search space of planar topological arrangements; (2)~a \textit{testing} phase that identified motor commands maximizing disagreement among competing models (active exploration); and (3)~a \textit{prediction} phase that used the best model to synthesize forward locomotion. After approximately 16 modeling-testing cycles involving roughly 250,000 internal simulations and 16 physical actions, the robot converged on an accurate morphological model and generated compensatory gaits after unexpected leg removal.

The model-driven algorithm achieved a 43.3\% success rate in inferring correct topology (versus 23.3\% and 26.7\% for random baselines), with mean model error of 7.31~$\pm$~1.22~cm before damage and 2.17~$\pm$~0.55~cm after damage (compared to 5.60~$\pm$~2.98~cm for baselines) \cite{bongard2006resilient}. A positive correlation between model disagreement and model error (Spearman $r = 0.425$, $P < 0.02$) validated the active exploration strategy.

While appealing, this approach suffers from the \textit{symbolic bottleneck}: the robot can only discover models constructible from predefined physics engine primitives (cylinders, hinge joints). It cannot represent non-rigid deformations, soft-body dynamics, or arbitrary visual damage---limitations that become critical in unstructured environments where failure modes are unpredictable.

\subsection{Behavioral Adaptation Without Explicit Models}

Recognizing the difficulty of explicit physical modeling, Cully et al.\ \cite{cully2015robots} introduced \textit{Intelligent Trial and Error} (IT\&E), which bypassed damage diagnosis entirely. Their key insight was to focus on \textit{finding what still works} rather than diagnosing what went wrong. Using the MAP-Elites algorithm \cite{mouret2015illuminating}, they pre-computed a behavioral repertoire of approximately 13,000 distinct gaits for a hexapod robot (18 motors, 36 gait parameters) through roughly 40 million simulations. This repertoire is a high-dimensional grid where each cell stores the highest-performing controller for a specific behavioral descriptor (e.g., duty cycle versus leg offset).

When damaged in the field, the robot treated this map as a Bayesian prior, using Gaussian Process regression to search for a behavior that performs well under the new morphological conditions. Cully et al.\ demonstrated that the hexapod recovered compensatory gaits in under two minutes of real-world testing across five damage conditions, including shortened legs, unpowered legs, and completely missing legs. Undamaged gaits were 30\% faster than a reference tripod gait (0.32~m/s versus 0.24~m/s), and damaged compensatory gaits achieved 3--7$\times$ better performance than the reference gait under damage \cite{cully2015robots}.

However, IT\&E represents a strategy of \textit{avoidance rather than adaptation}. The robot does not repair its internal model---it discards the parts of the behavioral space that no longer function. While effective for locomotion, this limits utility in manipulation tasks requiring precise kinematic accuracy. Furthermore, as robotic complexity increases, the curse of dimensionality makes pre-computing dense behavioral maps prohibitively expensive: a 6-DOF arm with even modest discretization could require billions of map entries.

\subsection{Task-Agnostic Deep Self-Models}

Kwiatkowski and Lipson \cite{kwiatkowski2019task} introduced the concept of \textit{task-agnostic self-modeling machines}, demonstrating a robot that creates a self-model with no prior knowledge of physics, kinematics, or its own shape, then reuses this model for multiple unrelated downstream tasks. A physical robot with 4 coupled degrees of freedom recorded 1,000 random action-sensation trajectories through motor babbling, where actions were motor angle commands and sensations were absolute end-effector coordinates.

A deep neural network was trained as an implicit self-model capturing the relationship between motor commands and end-effector position. The system was tested on two separate tasks---pick-and-place (9 red balls, 20~mm diameter) and handwriting---achieving a median closed-loop physical error of 0.6~cm (compared to 0.65~cm for an explicit analytical model and 4.3~cm for open-loop control). When a robot arm was replaced with a longer, slightly deformed part, the robot detected the change and retrained using only 10\% additional data, resuming operation with minimal performance loss \cite{kwiatkowski2019task}.

This work marked a shift from symbolic physics to learnable physics, demonstrating that a single learned model could serve multiple tasks without task-specific engineering. However, it only modeled end-effector position---not full-body morphology---limiting its applicability to tasks requiring whole-body awareness such as collision avoidance and self-inspection.

\section{Visual Self-Modeling via Neural Representations}
\label{sec:visual_selfmodel}
% ============================================================

The advent of deep learning and neural implicit representations enabled a key advance: robots could now model their \textit{entire body} in 3D, not merely end-effector positions.

\subsection{Full-Body Visual Self-Modeling with Signed Distance Functions}

Chen et al.\ \cite{chen2022full} proposed the first full-body visual self-model using neural implicit representations. Their system trained a Signed Distance Function (SDF) network to answer space occupancy queries conditioned on joint angles: given a 3D query point $\mathbf{x}$ and joint configuration $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, the network outputs whether the point lies inside or outside the robot body. The architecture comprised a coordinate network, a kinematic network, and a fusion MLP, using Sine activations (SIREN) for high-resolution 3D detail.

A critical distinction from prior work was the training paradigm: the model was trained as an Eikonal boundary value problem, requiring only point clouds and surface normals from fused sparse RGB-D views rather than ground-truth SDF values. Data collection used five Intel RealSense D435i depth cameras mounted around a WidowX 200 Robot Arm, capturing 10,000 data points in simulation and 7,888 in the real world \cite{chen2022full}.

The system achieved end-effector prediction error of 0.5~cm with a pretrained kinematic sub-network (versus 1.3~cm training from scratch), collision-free trajectory planning with a 96.84\% success rate, and model size of only 1.1~MB. For damage recovery, the model detected morphological changes by comparing prediction errors, identified the nature of damage through joint angle inverse inference, and recovered by fine-tuning with approximately 50 new observations, converging in 100 epochs ($\sim$8.13 minutes) \cite{chen2022full}.

This work established the occupancy-query paradigm for robot self-models but relied on five calibrated depth cameras, introducing significant hardware complexity and calibration brittleness.

\subsection{Free-Form Kinematic Self-Models Using Neural Radiance Fields}

Hu, Lin, and Lipson \cite{hu2024teaching} addressed the multi-camera limitation by proposing the Free-Form Kinematic Self-Model (FFKSM), which learns morphology, kinematics, and motor control from a single RGB camera through self-supervised learning. FFKSM adapts Neural Radiance Field concepts to articulated robots, conditioning occupancy prediction on joint angles. The architecture employs three neural networks: a \textit{Coordinates Encoder} mapping 3D points via positional encoding (5 frequencies, producing 33-dimensional representations), a \textit{Kinematic Encoder} processing the remaining two joint angles via positional encoding (5 frequencies, producing 22-dimensional representations), and a \textit{Predictive Module} that fuses these representations to output density and visibility.

A key innovation is the \textit{virtual frame prior}, which transforms 3D query points using the first two joint angles before network processing, reducing the learning burden by aligning coordinates with the robot's base orientation. The model requires only 333~KB of memory, three times smaller than Chen et al.'s approach \cite{chen2022full}---and performs inverse kinematics via gradient-based optimization without explicit kinematic equations.

Hu et al.\ validated the approach on two 4-DOF robot arms in both simulation and real-world settings, using 12,000 data points (10,000 for training/validation, 2,000 for testing). The system demonstrated spiral trajectory tracking, collision-free motion planning using RRT, and damage recovery through fine-tuning with post-damage data (tested with 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000 data points) \cite{hu2024teaching}.

While FFKSM substantially reduces sensor requirements, it inherits NeRF's fundamental computational limitation: volumetric rendering requires approximately 640,000 network evaluations per $100 \times 100$ image (10,000 rays $\times$ 64 samples per ray), resulting in inference speeds of approximately 5~FPS---far below the 1~kHz rates required for high-frequency control loops.

Moreover, the single-camera paradigm introduces depth ambiguity and occlusion vulnerability. Monocular setups cannot resolve the inherent scale-depth ambiguity of 2D projections, and self-occluded links remain invisible to the model regardless of joint configuration. Hu et al.\ themselves acknowledge this limitation, noting the ``reliance on 2D images for quantitative evaluations'' \cite{hu2024teaching}. Robust self-modeling for closed-loop control will likely require multi-view sensing or active viewpoint selection to provide reliable depth estimation and complete geometric coverage across the robot's full workspace.

Schulze and Lipson \cite{schulze2024high} addressed the challenge of scaling neural self-models to higher degrees of freedom. They proposed a dynamic neural density field architecture with \textit{DOF-individual encoders}---per-joint MLPs that process each joint angle independently before concatenation---and \textit{group encoders} that fuse spatial and DOF information. Notably, they found that sinusoidal positional encoding \textit{hindered} learning in high-DOF settings, contrary to its benefit in standard NeRF applications.

A critical contribution was \textit{curricular data sampling}: training data was generated using the powerset of DOF indices sorted by ascending magnitude, enabling the model to learn each DOF's contribution individually before encountering increasingly complex combinations. This strategy was validated on a 7-DOF Panda robot simulated in PyBullet (with a rotatable base, yielding 8 effective DOFs), using 5,588 annotated $400 \times 400$ images.

The model achieved a mean Chamfer-L2 distance of 1.94\% of workspace dimension on the test set, with individual configurations ranging from 1.06\% to 4.22\%. Downstream tasks including inverse kinematics via projected gradient descent and RRT-based collision-avoidance motion planning were successfully demonstrated \cite{schulze2024high}. This work bridges the gap between 4-DOF proof-of-concept systems and practical higher-DOF applications, though the single-camera setup and binary silhouette representation remain limitations for real-world deployment.

\subsection{Egocentric Self-Modeling for Dynamics Prediction}

While the preceding approaches use external cameras, Hu, Chen, and Lipson \cite{hu2024egocentric} pioneered \textit{egocentric} visual self-modeling, where a robot learns a dynamic self-model using only a single first-person-view camera. Their system was tested on a 12-DOF legged robot (4 legs, 3 motors per leg), using a hybrid residual network plus LSTM architecture that processes sequences of five consecutive $128 \times 128$ grayscale images alongside 12 motor angle commands to predict 6-DOF body state changes ($\Delta x$, $\Delta y$, $\Delta z$, $\Delta$roll, $\Delta$pitch, $\Delta$yaw).

This work extends self-modeling from kinematic reconstruction to \textit{dynamics prediction}---the model learns not just what the robot looks like, but how it moves. Extensive domain randomization (observation, action, friction, terrain textures) enabled sim-to-real transfer. The system outperformed action-only and IMU-only baselines across all locomotion tasks and terrains, with prediction errors of 1.30--1.90$\times 10^{-3}$ versus 1.90--3.10$\times 10^{-3}$ for action-only baselines.

Transfer learning experiments demonstrated that the pretrained visual encoder successfully transferred to three additional robot morphologies (including one with 90$^\circ$ rotated joints) using 50\% less data. Damage recovery was validated by detecting anomalies from discrepancies between predicted and actual motion, then retraining with 30 minutes of real-world motor babbling data \cite{hu2024egocentric}. This work shows that self-modeling can work with egocentric rather than external cameras and can learn dynamics (not just kinematics), extending the paradigm to mobile robots.

\subsection{Articulated 3D Gaussian Splatting for Robot Self-Modeling}

Most recently, Hu, Yu, and Tan \cite{hu2025learning} proposed one of the first 3D Gaussian Splatting-based robot self-modeling methods, addressing FFKSM's quality limitations. Their approach models robot morphology, kinematics, \textit{and surface color} at the link level---addressing a dimension of self-modeling (surface appearance) that prior methods had neglected. The architecture constructs a static 3DGS model, applies K-means clustering to segment Gaussians, introduces neural ellipsoid bones, and employs a kinematic neural network (MLP encoder + multi-head attention transformer + MLP decoder) with Linear Blend Skinning (LBS) for articulated deformation.

The method requires only multi-view RGB images, joint angles, and camera parameters---no depth sensors. On a simulated 7-DOF Franka robot (10,000 images), it achieved PSNR of 31.22~dB, SSIM of 0.988, and LPIPS of 0.0137. On a physical 4-DOF OpenManipulator ($\sim$1,100 images), it successfully reproduced morphology and surface color. The rendering time was approximately 0.08 seconds per image. Downstream tasks including target reaching, collision-free motion planning, and inverse kinematics were demonstrated without additional training \cite{hu2025learning}.

This work outperforms both Chen et al.\ \cite{chen2022full} and Schulze and Lipson \cite{schulze2024high} in geometric accuracy (lower Chamfer distance) for most configurations, representing the current state of the art in visual fidelity for robot self-models. However, training requires 16--24 hours on an RTX 3090, and the dense kinematic network retains vulnerability to catastrophic interference.

\section{Neural Rendering Foundations}
\label{sec:neural_rendering}
% ============================================================

The visual self-modeling approaches described above are built upon neural rendering techniques that have transformed 3D scene representation. This section reviews the foundational methods and their relevance to robot self-modeling.

\subsection{Neural Radiance Fields}

Mildenhall et al.\ \cite{mildenhall2020nerf} introduced Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF), representing scenes as continuous 5D functions $F_\Theta(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{d}) \rightarrow (\mathbf{c}, \sigma)$ that map spatial position $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and viewing direction $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{S}^2$ to color $\mathbf{c}$ and volume density $\sigma$. The architecture uses 8 fully-connected layers with 256 channels, where density depends only on position (ensuring multiview consistency) while color depends on both position and direction.

Two innovations were critical: \textit{positional encoding} $\gamma(\mathbf{p}) = [\sin(2^0 \pi \mathbf{p}), \cos(2^0 \pi \mathbf{p}), \ldots, \sin(2^{L-1} \pi \mathbf{p}), \cos(2^{L-1} \pi \mathbf{p})]$ with $L{=}10$ for position and $L{=}4$ for direction to overcome spectral bias, and \textit{hierarchical volume sampling} using coarse ($N_c{=}64$) and fine ($N_f{=}128$) networks. NeRF achieved PSNR of 31.01~dB on realistic synthetic scenes, outperforming prior methods by 5+ dB, with model size of only $\sim$5~MB \cite{mildenhall2020nerf}. However, training requires 1--2 days on an NVIDIA V100, and rendering requires dense ray-marching along each pixel.

Pumarola et al.\ \cite{pumarola2021d} extended NeRF to dynamic scenes with D-NeRF, introducing a decomposition into a \textit{canonical network} encoding the scene in a reference configuration and a \textit{deformation network} predicting displacement fields that map observations at time $t$ to canonical space. This decomposition closely parallels robot self-modeling, where joint angles induce deformations from a reference pose. D-NeRF achieved PSNR improvements of up to 15~dB over naive NeRF on dynamic scenes with articulated motion, though improvements varied by scene (from 1.3~dB to 14.6~dB) \cite{pumarola2021d}.

Several works have addressed NeRF's computational bottleneck. M\"{u}ller et al.\ \cite{muller2022instant} introduced Instant Neural Graphics Primitives (Instant-NGP) with a multiresolution hash encoding that replaces positional encoding with trainable feature vectors stored in $L{=}16$ levels of hash tables. This achieves competitive quality with NeRF in approximately 15 seconds of training (versus 1--2 days) and renders at 60 FPS at HD resolution, representing a 20--60$\times$ improvement over frequency encoding \cite{muller2022instant}. Barron et al.\ \cite{barron2021mip} proposed Mip-NeRF, which casts cones instead of rays and uses Integrated Positional Encoding to reason about conical frustums, reducing error rates by 17\% on standard datasets and 60\% on multiscale variants while merging NeRF's two networks into one (50\% size reduction) \cite{barron2021mip}.

For robot self-modeling, these acceleration techniques suggest that the NeRF-based self-model training pipeline could potentially be reduced from hours to minutes, enabling near-real-time model updates as the robot explores new configurations.

\subsection{3D Gaussian Splatting}

Kerbl et al.\ \cite{kerbl20233d} introduced 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), an explicit alternative to implicit NeRFs that represents scenes as discrete anisotropic 3D Gaussians. Each Gaussian is parameterized by position $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, full 3D covariance $\Sigma$ (decomposed into scaling $\mathbf{s}$ and rotation quaternion $\mathbf{q}$), opacity $\alpha \in [0,1]$, and view-dependent color encoded via spherical harmonics. The 3D covariance projects to 2D via the viewing transformation:
\begin{equation}
\Sigma' = \mathbf{J} \mathbf{W} \Sigma \mathbf{W}^T \mathbf{J}^T
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{W}$ is the world-to-camera transform and $\mathbf{J}$ is the projection Jacobian.

The key innovation is a tile-based differentiable rasterizer that splits the screen into $16 \times 16$ tiles, performs GPU radix sort by depth, and alpha-blends Gaussians front-to-back. Adaptive density control clones under-reconstructed and splits over-reconstructed Gaussians during optimization. On the Mip-NeRF360 dataset, 3DGS achieved PSNR of 27.21~dB at 134~FPS rendering---matching or exceeding Mip-NeRF360 quality while being over 1,000$\times$ faster at rendering. Training completes in $\sim$41 minutes versus $\sim$48 hours for Mip-NeRF360 \cite{kerbl20233d}.

Two SLAM systems have extended 3DGS to real-time operation. Matsuki et al.\ \cite{matsuki2024gaussian} developed the first monocular SLAM system using 3DGS as the sole representation, running live at 3~FPS. They derived analytical SE(3) Jacobians for camera pose optimization and introduced isotropic regularization to prevent degenerate Gaussians, achieving ATE RMSE of 1.47~cm on TUM RGB-D. GauRast achieves a 23$\times$ speedup and 24$\times$ energy efficiency improvement for Gaussian rasterization, bringing end-to-end 3DGS to 24--46~FPS on edge SoCs at only 0.2\% additional SoC area \cite{lassanske2024gaurast}. \section{3D Gaussian Splatting in Robotics}
\label{sec:3dgs_robotics}
% ============================================================

The explicit, differentiable nature of 3DGS has spurred numerous robotics applications, from articulated object modeling to manipulation planning. This section surveys the rapidly expanding intersection of 3DGS and robotics.

\subsection{Articulated Object Modeling}

Two recent works have demonstrated self-supervised digital twin construction for articulated objects using 3DGS---a capability directly transferable to robot self-modeling.

Guo et al.\ \cite{guo2025articulatedgs} proposed ArticulatedGS, a self-supervised framework requiring only two sets of multi-view RGB images in different articulation states---no depth, segmentation, or CAD models. The method uses a DeformNet to predict deformation flow, identifies movable Gaussians via thresholding, and optimizes motion parameters for revolute (axis, pivot, angle) and prismatic (axis, distance) joints. On the PARIS dataset, ArticulatedGS achieved angular errors as low as 0.02$^\circ$ (versus 7.90$^\circ$ for PARIS and 89.35$^\circ$ for Ditto) with approximately one-third the optimization time of competing methods \cite{guo2025articulatedgs}.

Yu et al.\ \cite{yu2025artgs} developed ArtGS, a three-component framework that constructs static 3DGS reconstructions of both robots and objects, uses a fine-tuned Vision-Language Model (InternVL-2.5-4B) for joint parameter initialization, and performs dynamic 3DGS articulation modeling with Linear Blend Skinning and impedance control. Of particular interest, the robot itself is transformed into a 3DGS digital twin using Modified Denavit-Hartenberg (MDH) parameters. On manipulating articulated objects, ArtGS achieved manipulation success rates of 62--90\% across seven categories, substantially outperforming baselines such as TD3, Where2Act, and VAT-Mart (3--41\%) as well as the strongest baseline GAMMA (42--57\%), with successful real-world deployment on both Franka and xArm7 robots \cite{yu2025artgs}.



\textbf{Broad Applications in Articulated Objects:} Beyond the core works discussed initially, dozens of other parallel models investigate detailed kinematic joint inference, neural part-based segmentation, and active interaction tracking strategies for manipulating articulated objects \cite{sup_ARTArticulatedR5676,sup_CA2TNetCategory9713,sup_DragMeshInterac8443,sup_FreeGaussianAnn2942,sup_Real2Render2Rea8786,sup_REArtGSGenerali1128,sup_SGVLALearningSp4926,sup_SIMARTDecomposi5433}.

\subsection{Physics-Consistent Robot Models}

Beyond visual reconstruction, several works have sought to imbue Gaussian robot models with physical consistency.

Lou et al.\ \cite{lou2024robogs} proposed Robo-GS, which establishes an isomorphic \textit{Gaussian-Mesh-Pixel binding}---a three-way mapping between mesh vertices, Gaussian kernels, and image pixels. Using 2DGS surfel representation for mesh extraction and kinematic control via MDH parameters, Robo-GS further incorporates Newton-Euler dynamics for physics simulation. This hybrid representation achieved an F-score of 0.721 (versus 0.268--0.375 for alternatives) and supports compatibility with physics simulators (Isaac Sim/Gym) for sim-to-real transfer across CR3, CR5, and UR5 robots \cite{lou2024robogs}.

Sun et al.\ \cite{sun2026digitaltwin} presented an end-to-end digital twin pipeline that combines 3DGS reconstruction from sparse views (10--20 images) with visibility-aware semantic fusion, physics-ready geometry extraction, and Unity-ROS2-MoveIt integration. The system achieved PSNR of 37.03$\pm$5.0~dB (versus 28.33~dB for NeRF) with reconstruction time of 229$\pm$120~seconds (5$\times$ faster than NeRF). Real-world manipulation trials achieved a 90\% success rate with 0.83~cm average placement error and zero collisions \cite{sun2026digitaltwin}.

\subsection{Gaussian Splatting for Manipulation}

The predictive capabilities of Gaussian representations have been exploited for manipulation planning and execution.

Lu et al.\ \cite{lu2024manigaussian} proposed ManiGaussian, which combines a Gaussian Scene Regressor with a Deformation Predictor to build a ``Gaussian World Model'' that anticipates future scene states conditioned on robot actions. On RLBench (10 tasks, 166 variations), ManiGaussian achieved 44.8\% average success rate, outperforming previous state-of-the-art GNFactor (31.7\%) by 13.1 percentage points with 2.29$\times$ faster training \cite{lu2024manigaussian}.

Pan et al.\ \cite{pan2025selfcorrecting} extended this paradigm with foresight-driven failure detection: a prediction network estimates future Gaussian scene states, and Chamfer distance between predicted and observed point clouds triggers self-correction via rollback with Gaussian perturbation. This achieved 56.4\% success rate on PerAct tasks (+12.0 percentage points over ManiGaussian), with the self-correction mechanism contributing +18.4 percentage points \cite{pan2025selfcorrecting}. This is fundamentally a self-model used for action monitoring and recovery---the robot builds an internal predictive model, detects divergence from reality, and self-corrects.

Yang et al.\ \cite{yang2025robosplat} proposed RoboSplat, which generates diverse manipulation demonstrations from a single expert trajectory via 3DGS scene editing. Using the robot's URDF as a self-model for link-wise Gaussian segmentation and differentiable frame alignment, RoboSplat achieved 87.8\% average success rate across six generalization types from one-shot learning---versus 57.2\% for policies trained on hundreds of real-world demonstrations. Cross-embodiment transfer (Franka to UR5e) was demonstrated, with data generation running 29$\times$ faster than real-world collection \cite{yang2025robosplat}.

Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2025actron3d} developed Actron3D, which distills neural affordance functions from 2--3 demonstration videos, encoding geometry (SDF), color, visual features (DINO descriptors), contact probability, and action flows as continuous neural fields. On 13 RLBench tasks, Actron3D achieved 90.8\% success rate---the highest reported---using only human demonstration videos without robot-specific data \cite{zhang2025actron3d}.

Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2026exogs} proposed ExoGS, a robot-free data collection framework using a passive exoskeleton for demonstration capture and 3DGS for scene reconstruction. URDF-based forward kinematics renders the robot into generated scenes, enabling dramatic generalization improvements: 76\% success on novel objects versus 0\% for baselines \cite{wang2026exogs}.

\section{Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap}
\label{sec:sim2real}
% ============================================================

Robot self-modeling is closely linked to the sim-to-real transfer problem: an accurate self-model effectively serves as an internal simulation of the robot itself, potentially reducing the reality gap from within.

\subsection{Understanding the Reality Gap}

Aljalbout et al.\ \cite{aljalbout2026realitygap} provide the most thorough recent survey of the sim-to-real problem, formalizing the reality gap using a POMDP framework and distinguishing between the \textit{dynamics gap} (differences in physics simulation) and the \textit{perception gap} (differences in sensor models and visual appearance). They identify four main sources: dynamics (integrators, parameterization, robot modeling), perception and sensing (sensor models, noise), actuation and control (actuator models, low-level control), and system design (construction differences).

The survey notes that modern GPU-parallelized simulators can simulate thousands of robots simultaneously, and that perfect fidelity is ``impractical and computationally infeasible''---successful transfer can occur if the policy is robust against environment differences \cite{aljalbout2026realitygap}. Self-modeling directly addresses the dynamics and embodiment components of this gap by learning the robot's actual physical properties rather than relying on potentially inaccurate simulation parameters.

\subsection{Real-to-Sim-to-Real Pipelines}

Several recent systems demonstrate how accurate 3D reconstruction can bridge the reality gap, supporting the case for automatic self-model construction.

Torne et al.\ \cite{torne2024rialto} proposed RialTo, which constructs digital twins from real-world data via 3D reconstruction, performs inverse distillation to transfer real demonstrations into simulation, applies RL fine-tuning with sparse rewards, and deploys through teacher-student distillation with real data co-training. Non-expert users could construct digital twins in under 15 minutes, and RialTo achieved over 67\% improvement in policy robustness over baselines \cite{torne2024rialto}.

Yu et al.\ \cite{yu2025real2render2real} introduced Real2Render2Real (R2R2R), which generates robot training data from a smartphone-captured scan and a single human demonstration video using 3DGS for object reconstruction and 4D Differentiable Part Modeling for motion tracking. Policies trained on R2R2R data from a single demonstration matched the performance of 150 teleoperation demonstrations, generating data at 51 demonstrations per minute (27$\times$ faster than teleoperation) \cite{yu2025real2render2real}.

Two complementary frameworks address policy evaluation in simulation. Jain et al.\ \cite{jain2025polaris} proposed PolaRiS, which uses 2D Gaussian Splatting to reconstruct real-world scenes and automatic articulation of robot Gaussian splats based on URDF forward kinematics, achieving Pearson correlation $r = 0.9$ with real-world policy performance. Li et al.\ \cite{li2024simpler} developed SIMPLER, which mitigates the visual gap through green-screening and texture matching, achieving Pearson $r = 0.924$ across Google Robot tasks (up to $r = 0.976$ on individual tasks) \cite{li2024simpler}.

Zhang et al.\ \cite{zhang2025softbody} extended real-to-sim evaluation to soft-body interactions by combining 3DGS for photorealistic rendering with PhysTwin for deformable object dynamics (dense spring-mass systems), achieving Pearson $r > 0.9$ across all tasks including deformable objects---a previously unaddressed domain \cite{zhang2025softbody}.

\subsection{Online Correction and Adaptation}

An alternative to building accurate simulations is learning to correct for the sim-to-real gap during deployment. Jiang et al.\ \cite{jiang2024transic} proposed TRANSIC, where human operators observe sim-trained policy execution on real robots and intervene via teleoperation, with correction data training a residual policy. TRANSIC achieved 77\% average success rate across five deliberately exacerbated sim-to-real gaps using only 17--100 correction trajectories \cite{jiang2024transic}.

In the dexterous manipulation domain, a recent work \cite{bytedance2025closing} demonstrated zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for force-based grasping on a 12-DOF dexterous hand by combining fast tactile simulation (600 virtual tactile units), current-to-torque calibration, and actuator dynamics modeling with extensive randomization. This amounts to a practical form of self-modeling: the calibration process maps the real robot's physical properties (motor current-torque relationships, backlash thresholds) into the simulation, effectively learning the robot's own actuator characteristics \cite{bytedance2025closing}.

\section{Damage Recovery and Fault Tolerance}
\label{sec:damage_recovery}
% ============================================================

A primary motivation for robot self-modeling is enabling autonomous damage recovery. Beyond the self-model-based approaches reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:visual_selfmodel}, two complementary paradigms have emerged.

Fu et al.\ \cite{fu2025contrastive} proposed a reinforcement learning framework integrating contrastive learning with a forward prediction model for adaptive fault-tolerant legged robots. The forward model predicts the robot's subsequent state---effectively a self-model---and prediction errors between expected and actual states serve as signals for detecting damage. Contrastive learning in the latent space enables the policy to distinguish between different joint damage conditions, as confirmed by t-SNE analysis. The method reduced velocity tracking error by 41.7\% for zero-torque damage and 61.5\% for locked-joint damage compared to baselines, with zero-shot transfer capability between damage types and validation on both quadruped and hexapod robots across diverse terrains \cite{fu2025contrastive}.

Chen et al.\ \cite{chen2024vlm} approached failure recovery through Vision-Language Models (VLMs), using GPT-4o as a black-box controller for both motion-level position correction and task-level recovery. Through optimized visual and language prompts, they achieved 65.78\% coverage in target reach tasks (versus 0\% with unoptimized prompts) on a 7-DOF Kinova robot \cite{chen2024vlm}. This VLM-based approach provides a complementary perspective: rather than building internal self-models, it uses external foundation models for reasoning about the robot's state and failures.

These approaches highlight a spectrum from fully internal self-models (FFKSM, 3DGS-based) to hybrid systems (forward prediction + contrastive learning) to externally grounded reasoning (VLMs). Each has different trade-offs in latency, autonomy, and generalization capability.

\section{Continual Learning for Self-Model Maintenance}
\label{sec:continual_learning}
% ============================================================

When a robot sustains damage and must update its self-model, a critical challenge arises: the model must learn new configurations (plasticity) while retaining knowledge of undamaged components (stability). This is the \textit{stability-plasticity dilemma}, a fundamental tension in neural network learning with deep biological roots.

\subsection{Catastrophic Forgetting}

McCloskey and Cohen \cite{mccloskey1989catastrophic} first demonstrated catastrophic interference in connectionist networks: a backpropagation network trained on ``one's addition facts'' then ``two's addition facts'' lost recall from 100\% to 0\% within 15 trials. French \cite{french1999catastrophic} provided a thorough review, identifying the root cause as \textit{shared distributed representations}---the same weight sharing that enables generalization causes catastrophic erasure when weights are updated for new patterns.

French identified several solution families: reducing representational overlap through orthogonal recoding, modular architectures with localized receptive fields, rehearsal techniques (including Robins' pseudopattern technique, which interleaves random input-output pairs to approximate the learned function), and dual-network models inspired by the hippocampal-neocortical separation theory \cite{french1999catastrophic}.

In the context of robot self-modeling, catastrophic forgetting manifests as follows: when a robot with a damaged leg fine-tunes its self-model on new damage data, gradient updates through shared hidden layers simultaneously degrade the model's predictions for healthy limbs. The robot learns its leg is broken but forgets how to reach with its arm---an unacceptable failure mode.

\subsection{The Stability-Plasticity Dilemma}

Grossberg \cite{grossberg1987competitive} first articulated the stability-plasticity dilemma in the context of competitive learning and Adaptive Resonance Theory. Mermillod, Bugaiska, and Bonin \cite{mermillod2013stability} later framed catastrophic forgetting and the entrenchment effect (age-of-acquisition effects) as two extremes of the same stability-plasticity continuum. They demonstrated that the Fahlman offset---a small constant added to the derivative of the sigmoid activation---modulates network plasticity: with offset 0.1, age-limited learning effects disappear (plasticity maintained); with offset 0.0, entrenchment reappears \cite{mermillod2013stability}.

For robot self-models, this continuum implies that tuning between these extremes is essential: too much plasticity causes forgetting of healthy configurations; too much stability prevents learning new damaged morphology. The ideal self-model architecture should enable \textit{selective plasticity}---allowing localized updates to damage-affected components while maintaining stability of unaffected parts.

\subsection{Regularization-Based Approaches}

Kirkpatrick et al.\ \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} proposed Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), which selectively slows learning on weights important for previously learned tasks. EWC augments the loss for a new task with a quadratic penalty:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathcal{L}_B(\boldsymbol{\theta}) + \sum_i \frac{\lambda}{2} F_i (\theta_i - \theta_{A,i}^*)^2
\end{equation}
where $\theta_{A,i}^*$ are the optimal parameters for previous tasks and $F_i$ is the diagonal of the Fisher Information Matrix, estimating weight importance via loss landscape curvature. EWC successfully scaled to training a single DQN on 10 Atari games sequentially \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}.

While effective for classification tasks, EWC has limitations for real-time robot damage recovery. Computing the Fisher Matrix requires second-order derivatives, which is expensive for high-dimensional rendering networks. Furthermore, EWC treats the network as a monolith with varying stiffness---if damage is severe, the robot needs to change high-importance weights, but EWC prevents this, creating a ``plasticity gap.''

\subsection{Sparse and Modular Architectures}

The limitations of regularization-based approaches suggest that \textit{network topology}---rather than weight values---may be key to adaptive resilience.

Frankle and Carbin \cite{frankle2018lottery} formalized the \textit{Lottery Ticket Hypothesis}: dense, randomly initialized networks contain sparse subnetworks (``winning tickets'') that, when trained in isolation from their original initialization, match the full network's accuracy. Winning tickets were consistently found at 10--20\% of original network size across fully-connected and convolutional architectures, and they \textit{learned faster} and reached \textit{higher accuracy} than the original dense networks \cite{frankle2018lottery}. For robot self-models, this suggests that compact, pruned subnetworks could represent different body parts, enabling modular adaptation after damage.

Han, Mao, and Dally \cite{han2015deep} demonstrated practical compression through a three-stage pipeline: pruning (removing small weights), trained quantization (K-means clustering of weights), and Huffman coding. VGG-16 was compressed from 552~MB to 11.3~MB (49$\times$) with no accuracy loss, and compressed models fit in on-chip SRAM (5~pJ/access vs.\ 640~pJ for DRAM), yielding up to 128$\times$ lower per-access energy cost \cite{han2015deep}. For embedded robotic platforms (e.g., NVIDIA Jetson Orin at 15W), such compression enables deploying self-models within strict power and memory constraints.

Mocanu et al.\ \cite{mocanu2018scalable} introduced Sparse Evolutionary Training (SET), which maintains sparse topology \textit{throughout training} rather than pruning post-hoc. Starting from an Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi random graph, SET prunes the weakest connections and regrows random new ones each epoch, allowing the topology to evolve dynamically. The sparse connectivity evolves toward a scale-free topology (power-law degree distribution), mimicking biological neural networks. On CIFAR-10, SET-MLP achieved 74.84\% accuracy with only 1\% of fully-connected parameters \cite{mocanu2018scalable}.

This connection to biology is reinforced by Chechik, Meilijson, and Ruppin's \cite{chechik1998synaptic} computational models of synaptic pruning, which proved mathematically that pruning is an optimal strategy \textit{strictly under metabolic or resource constraints}. In the absence of energetic costs, pruning cannot improve network performance. This finding aligns precisely with edge computing constraints in robotics: under limited power budgets and memory bandwidth, sparse architectures are not merely beneficial but theoretically optimal.

% ============================================================
\section{Enabling Techniques}
\label{sec:enabling}
% ============================================================

Several adjacent research areas provide critical capabilities that complement and enhance robot self-modeling.

\subsection{Exploration Strategies for Self-Model Construction}

Building a self-model requires efficient exploration of the robot's joint space. Pathak et al.\ \cite{pathak2017curiosity} proposed the Intrinsic Curiosity Module (ICM), where curiosity is formulated as the prediction error of a forward dynamics model in a learned feature space. An inverse dynamics model learns features encoding only agent-relevant aspects (ignoring uncontrollable environmental variation), while a forward model predicts future features. This self-supervised approach achieved exploration in environments with sparse or no extrinsic rewards, including transfer of learned exploration behaviors to new environments \cite{pathak2017curiosity}.

For robot self-modeling, curiosity-driven exploration could replace random motor babbling with intelligent probing: the robot would preferentially explore joint configurations where its self-model is most uncertain, analogous to Bongard et al.'s active exploration strategy \cite{bongard2006resilient} but using learned features rather than symbolic model disagreement.

\subsection{Meta-Learning for Rapid Adaptation}

Finn, Abbeel, and Levine \cite{finn2017maml} introduced Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning (MAML), which trains a model's initial parameters such that a few gradient steps on a new task produce good generalization. The inner loop $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i' = \boldsymbol{\theta} - \alpha \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \mathcal{L}_{T_i}(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})$ adapts to a specific task, while the outer loop optimizes for post-adaptation performance across tasks. MAML adapted to new regression functions from $K{=}5$ datapoints and new RL tasks in 2--3 gradient steps \cite{finn2017maml}.

For robot self-modeling, MAML is directly applicable to damage recovery: a robot could meta-learn its self-model such that after damage, only a few gradient steps with new sensory data suffice to update the model. This ``fast adaptation'' paradigm could reduce the data requirements for post-damage model updates from thousands of samples to tens, enabling rapid recovery in the field.

\subsection{Embodied Intelligence and Dexterous Manipulation}

The ultimate value of robot self-models lies in enabling downstream tasks. He et al.\ \cite{he2024h2o} demonstrated Human-to-Humanoid (H2O) teleoperation, enabling real-time whole-body control of a Unitree H1 humanoid using only an RGB camera. The system uses a sim-to-data pipeline that retargets human motions via inverse kinematics, trains a privileged motion imitator, and distills to a real-time policy with extensive domain randomization. The 72.5\% simulation success rate and successful real-world demonstrations (walking, jumping, kicking, boxing) highlight both the potential and the challenges of embodiment transfer \cite{he2024h2o}.

An et al.\ \cite{an2025dexterous} provide a detailed survey of imitation learning for dexterous manipulation, reviewing data collection methods (teleoperation, pose estimation), end-effectors (multi-fingered hands, anthropomorphic arms), and learning paradigms (behavior cloning, hybrid RL+IL, inverse RL). A key finding is that imitation learning is particularly effective for dexterous manipulation because objects are designed for human hands, creating a natural mapping. However, several challenges remain open: scalability of data collection, generalization to novel objects, and the sim-to-real gap \cite{an2025dexterous}. Self-modeling directly addresses these challenges by providing accurate body representations that enable precise retargeting and control---if the self-model is inaccurate (e.g., after damage), both retargeting and control fail.

\section{Discussion: Open Challenges and Future Directions}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ============================================================

Despite significant advances, several fundamental challenges remain in robot self-modeling. We organize these into five categories and discuss promising research directions for each.

\subsection{Real-Time Inference and Edge Deployment}

Current self-models remain computationally expensive. FFKSM achieves approximately 5~FPS \cite{hu2024teaching}, and articulated 3DGS requires $\sim$0.08 seconds per image \cite{hu2025learning}. For integration into high-frequency control loops (1~kHz for force control, 100~Hz for impedance control), inference latency must be reduced to sub-millisecond levels. Promising directions include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Direct sensorimotor decoding}: bypassing 3D reconstruction entirely by directly mapping joint angles to visual predictions, which has demonstrated over 1,000$\times$ speedup in preliminary studies.
    \item \textbf{Hardware-accelerated rendering}: GauRast \cite{lassanske2024gaurast} demonstrates that minor hardware modifications (0.2\% SoC area) can achieve 23$\times$ speedup for Gaussian rasterization on edge devices.
    \item \textbf{Model compression}: applying deep compression techniques \cite{han2015deep} and sparse evolutionary training \cite{mocanu2018scalable} to self-model networks for deployment on power-constrained platforms (15W NVIDIA Jetson Orin).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scaling to Complex Morphologies}

While early self-modeling research has frequently relied on simplified 4-DOF platforms, scaling to standard 6-DOF industrial manipulators is a necessary intermediate step. Six-DOF configurations are required to handle the complex manipulation and spatial orientation tasks encountered in real-world deployment, and their kinematic complexity provides a meaningful test of architectural scalability. Beyond 6-DOF, extending to high-DOF systems (humanoid robots with 30+ joints, soft robots with infinite DOF) introduces the curse of dimensionality: covering the joint space requires exponentially more training data. Schulze and Lipson's curricular data sampling \cite{schulze2024high} and Hu et al.'s egocentric approach \cite{hu2024egocentric} represent initial steps, but fundamental architectural innovations are needed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical encoders} that decompose complex morphologies into limbs, torso, and extremities with separate sub-models.
    \item \textbf{Graph neural networks} that exploit the robot's kinematic tree structure, processing each kinematic chain independently before fusion.
    \item \textbf{Attention mechanisms} that dynamically weight joint contributions based on their relevance to specific query points.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Real Hardware Validation and Sim-to-Real Transfer}

A persistent limitation is that many recent advances remain simulation-only \cite{schulze2024high}. The sim-to-real gap for self-models is particularly challenging because real images contain lighting variations, shadows, background clutter, and calibration errors absent in simulation. The growing ecosystem of real-to-sim-to-real tools \cite{torne2024rialto, yu2025real2render2real, jain2025polaris, zhang2025softbody} provides infrastructure for validation, but systematic real-world benchmarks for self-modeling are lacking. Self-models that can learn from real sensor data (with noise, occlusion, and varying illumination) and adapt to physical wear and deformation remain an open challenge.

\subsection{Catastrophic Forgetting in Damage Recovery}

When a robot sustains damage and must update its self-model, catastrophic forgetting threatens to destroy knowledge of healthy components. While EWC \cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}, lottery ticket pruning \cite{frankle2018lottery}, and sparse evolutionary training \cite{mocanu2018scalable} offer partial solutions, no method has been specifically designed and validated for robot self-model maintenance. Promising directions include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Modular self-models}: architectures where different body parts are encoded by physically separate network modules, enabling localized updates.
    \item \textbf{Mixture of experts}: maintaining separate expert networks for healthy versus damaged states, gated by anomaly detection scores.
    \item \textbf{Meta-learned initialization}: using MAML \cite{finn2017maml} to find self-model initializations that can rapidly adapt to new morphologies with minimal data.
    \item \textbf{Progressive neural networks}: growing the architecture to accommodate new morphological configurations without overwriting existing knowledge.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Multi-Modal and Multi-Use Self-Models}

Current self-models typically serve a single purpose (visual prediction or occupancy querying). Future self-models should integrate multiple modalities---vision, proprioception, tactile feedback, force sensing---and support multiple downstream tasks simultaneously: collision checking, damage detection, proprioceptive drift correction, motion planning, and control. The work of Pan et al.\ \cite{pan2025selfcorrecting} on predictive self-models for failure detection and the work on force-aware sim-to-real transfer \cite{bytedance2025closing} suggest that multi-modal self-models combining geometric and dynamic information are both feasible and necessary for robust autonomous operation.

\subsection{Critical Systemic Discrepancies and Emerging Solutions}

A rigorous analysis of recent self-modeling literature (2024--2026) reveals several systemic discrepancies between proposed capabilities and actual robotic deployment requirements. Most prominently, while methods like AOMGen \cite{aomgen2026} excel at generating visually realistic manipulation data through direct manipulations of 3D Gaussians, they fundamentally overlook the tactile friction and multi-contact dynamics necessary for generalized articulated interaction. The visual realism achieved via dynamic, differentiable 3DGS rendering, as seen in frameworks like ArtGS \cite{yu2025artgs}, does optimize articulated bone parameters to ensure physical constraints; however, these models require static multi-view captures that fail to account for severe dynamic occlusions occurring during dense clutter operations.

Recent frameworks such as AdaClearGrasp \cite{adacleargrasp2026} have attempted to bridge this by formulating manipulation as a controllable decision process coupled with Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to reason about grasp interference---yet these remain dependent on closed-loop feedback latencies that exceed the strict control frequencies (e.g., 1000 Hz) necessary for dynamic self-preservation. Furthermore, the push towards complex geometries with models like the Articulated Reconstruction Transformer (ART) \cite{art2026} demonstrates the capacity to map sparse images to learnable part slots for complete 3D kinematic tracking. Nevertheless, as highlighted by the AGILE comprehensive workflow for humanoid RL \cite{agile2026}, the critical bottleneck has transitioned away from simulation throughput directly toward the lack of standardized environment verification. Without a coherent loop coupling environment configuration descriptors to deployment, the sim-to-real gap persists as an ad-hoc engineering hurdle rather than a solved geometric abstraction. Consequently, future robust self-modeling architectures must prioritize real-time feature-embedded SLAM integration, similar to FeatureSLAM \cite{featureslam2026}, moving beyond purely visual metrics (PSNR, LPIPS) toward unified structural-semantic tracking frameworks.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

Robot self-modeling has evolved from symbolic estimation-exploration approaches to neural rendering-based visual models capable of high-fidelity 3D morphology reconstruction. This review has traced this evolution through three eras: the symbolic era pioneered by Bongard et al.\ \cite{bongard2006resilient}, the behavioral adaptation era exemplified by Cully et al.\ \cite{cully2015robots}, and the visual self-modeling era enabled by advances in neural implicit representations \cite{chen2022full, hu2024teaching, hu2025learning}.

The convergence of robot self-modeling with 3D Gaussian Splatting represents a promising research direction, offering explicit, differentiable representations that support real-time rendering and physics-consistent modeling. Simultaneously, the growing ecosystem of sim-to-real tools built on neural reconstruction demonstrates the practical value of accurate self-representations for policy transfer and evaluation.

The transition from simulated proofs-of-concept to deployed systems requires overcoming several strict operational barriers. Real-time inference suitable for high-frequency control loops requires architectural innovations beyond current neural rendering paradigms. Scaling from 4-DOF prototypes through 6-DOF industrial manipulators to high-DOF humanoid and soft robots demands hierarchical and graph-based architectures. Catastrophic forgetting during damage recovery necessitates modular or sparse network designs inspired by biological synaptic plasticity. And systematic real-world validation benchmarks, grounded in functional effectiveness rather than visual fidelity alone, are needed to move the field beyond simulation-only demonstrations.

To overcome the persistent neural rendering latency bottleneck, the field must move toward direct sensorimotor decoding. By bypassing expensive 3D volumetric reconstruction and directly mapping joint states to predictive outcomes, self-models can approach the sub-millisecond inference speeds required for real-time hardware control. Combined with multi-view sensing for reliable depth estimation, Gaussian Splatting for high-fidelity rendering where needed, and sparse modular architectures for continual learning under resource constraints, this direction offers the most promising path toward functionally effective and resilient autonomous robots capable of understanding, monitoring, and repairing their own bodies.
